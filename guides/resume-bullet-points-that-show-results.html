How to Write Resume Bullet Points That Show Results

A bullet should answer one question for the reader: so what? Here is a repeatable way to answer it honestly.

By Sumit Sengar | Published 11 October 2026 | Last updated 24 October 2026

The problem with most bullets

Most resume bullets describe a duty: "Responsible for preparing weekly reports". The reader learns what the job title already implied, not whether you did it well. A good bullet describes an action and its effect, so that two people with the same title look different on paper.

A formula you can reuse

Action verb + what you did + how much or how well, and optionally how.

This is a compressed version of the STAR method that the National Careers Service recommends for CVs: "Situation", "Task", "Action" and "Result - what happened as a result of your action and what you learned from the experience" (The STAR method). On a resume there is room for the action and the result; keep the situation and task for the interview. Its CV advice suggests "usually 2 to 3 lines" per job using this method (How to write a CV).

Before and after

The rewrites below are illustrative: the figures are invented to show the shape of a good bullet. Use only numbers you can explain in an interview.

BeforeAfter
Responsible for weekly sales reportsPrepared weekly sales reports in Excel for a 6-person team, cutting preparation time from three hours to one
Helped customers with queriesAnswered about 40 customer queries a day by phone and email, closing most the same day
Worked on college websiteBuilt a 12-page club website in HTML and CSS that 300 students used to register for events
Led a team in a college festLed 15 volunteers to run a two-day fest for 1,200 visitors, finishing under budget
Did data entryEntered and checked 250 records a day with fewer than two corrections a week
Made a project in PythonWrote a Python script that cleans exam result files, cutting manual checking from two hours to ten minutes

When you have no numbers

  • Count what you know: people in the team, pages, features, events, customers.
  • Use time: "in three weeks", "every Saturday for a semester".
  • Say who benefited: "used by the department", "adopted by the next batch".
  • Use a comparison: "replaced a paper register with a shared sheet".
  • Name the tool: "using Excel pivot tables", "in React".

If a bullet still has no result, ask what would have gone wrong without your work. That is usually the result.

Wording

  • Past tense for finished work; present tense only for a current role.
  • Vary the verbs: built, led, reduced, organised, analysed, trained.
  • One or two lines per bullet; three to five bullets for recent roles, fewer for older ones; strongest first.
  • Avoid "responsible for", "worked on" and "helped with" unless nothing else is true, and adjectives you cannot prove, such as "hardworking".
