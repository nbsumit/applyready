How to use this page

Pick Under 10 KB, Under 100 KB or Under 200 KB in the tool, or choose Custom size and type the exact minimum and maximum from your form. Then set the pixel size the form gives; the limit only decides how hard the compressor has to work. The sections below describe where each limit typically appears on job portals and application forms, and what the compressor produced on our synthetic test images (a 1200 × 1600 px portrait and a 1600 × 900 px phone photo of a signature, both in /img/samples/), measured in Chromium 141 on 24 October 2026. Jump to 10 KB, 100 KB or 200 KB. For the two most common limits there are dedicated pages: 20 KB and 50 KB.

10 KB: small signatures, and the minimum many forms set

Ten kilobytes (10,240 bytes) appears on application forms more often as a minimum than as a maximum: a signature field that accepts 10 to 20 KB, or a photo field that accepts 10 to 200 KB, rejects anything lighter than 10 KB. As a maximum, it is usually reserved for a small signature scan, for example a form that asks for a signature of 4.5 × 2 cm at 200 DPI under 10 KB.

A photo under 10 KB is only practical at small pixel sizes: at 600 × 800 px the quality had to drop to 0.53. If a form gives 10 KB as a minimum and your file is lighter, enter 10 in Minimum (KB) and the tool pads the JPEG to reach it.

100 KB: declarations, longer signatures and larger photos

One hundred kilobytes (102,400 bytes) is the ceiling for uploads that carry more ink or more pixels than a normal signature. The rows below are illustrative examples of the kind of rule you will see.

At 800 × 400 px, our signature photo came to 55.6 KB at full quality: inside a 50 to 100 KB window with no compression. A full page of handwriting has more ink than our test signature, so a real declaration may need slight compression, which 100 KB leaves room for. For declarations, write on plain white paper in black ink and crop close to the text before resizing.

200 KB: high-resolution photos and ID images

Two hundred kilobytes (204,800 bytes) is generous for a photo. It lets a portal ask for a large, sharp image while still keeping uploads small. A form might use a limit like this for the applicant's photo or for a JPEG of an ID card, sometimes as a range such as 20 to 200 KB, and sometimes without any pixel size.

With 200 KB to spend, even the full 1200 × 1600 photo kept a quality of 0.86. The risk at this limit is the other way round: a small 350 × 350 px photo was 21.6 KB, so check the form's minimum as well as its maximum.

If the portal still rejects the file

  • Below the minimum: enter the form's minimum in Minimum (KB); do not enlarge the image to gain bytes.
  • Wrong dimensions: the result table reads the pixel size from the finished file. If the form gives centimetres, read pixels, centimetres and DPI.
  • Over the limit by a few bytes: with 1 KB counted as 1,024 bytes, 100 KB is 102,400 bytes. Set the maximum 1 KB lower if unsure how a portal counts.
  • The photo looks soft: the pixel size is too large for the limit; see getting a photo under a KB limit without blur.
  • Anything else: match the portal's message in upload errors and their fixes.

Frequently asked questions

Which forms use a 10 KB, 100 KB or 200 KB limit?

Typically, 10 KB is a minimum for signatures and photos or a maximum for a small signature scan; 100 KB is used for handwritten declarations, longer signatures and some photos; 200 KB is used for higher-resolution photos and ID images. Each portal sets its own figure, so check the form.

Should I lower the quality or the pixel size?

The pixel size, first. Set the size the form asks for; the tool then keeps the highest quality that fits. In our tests a 1200 × 1600 photo needed quality 0.73 to fit 100 KB, but an 800 × 1000 photo kept 0.89.

What if my file is smaller than the form's minimum?

Enter the minimum in Minimum (KB). The tool pads the JPEG with comment data to reach it without changing the picture.

Is 100 KB the same as 100,000 bytes?

Not necessarily. With 1 KB counted as 1,024 bytes, 100 KB is 102,400 bytes. The result shows the exact byte count.

Is my file uploaded anywhere?

No. Everything runs in your browser on your device.
